The Platform That Lets Our Whole Design Team Prototype with AI

Exploring a design properly is expensive, so a feature gets one direction drawn at its happy path, and the states that actually decide it never get drawn at all. AI looked like the fix, and at first it made things worse: nearly right, which is the worst kind of wrong. And even once I’d got it working, it only worked for me.

So I built the Rayo Design Lab: a platform where AI builds inside our own design system, with the rules written where it can read them, and where any designer - no Terminal, no git, no code - can brief it, see several directions side by side in every state, and share the result. The team works in it now. A prototype that took days to draw and link in Figma is a brief and a conversation, and the time that frees goes on looking at more ideas before we choose.

My role

Design and development. I built the lab, designed the workflow and the Guide that get the team into it, and set up how work is checked and reviewed. I own the foundations every prototype sits on; the explorations are the team’s.

Impact

⛹️‍♂️ The whole team can build with AI, not just the person who set it up

Every designer on the team has set up from the Guide alone, without a session with me. The whole workflow is four commands, each with a button that opens Claude with the prompt already typed, and a button that messages me on Teams if it goes wrong.

⚡ Days of linking screens in Figma become a brief

A clickable prototype with its states used to mean days of drawing and linking screens, and re-linking them after every change. Now it’s a sentence or a pasted brief, a couple of questions, and a working prototype in the real components, in code where a state is a prop rather than another screen.

🗺️ A feature arrives as competing directions, not one frame

Every exploration starts as three directions sketched side by side - one close to today’s app, two bold - before anything is built out. Each option states what it’s betting and what it costs, in the frame, next to the thing it describes.

💡 New ideas land inside the product, not beside it

The lab keeps the app’s own screens as they actually are, so a new idea gets designed into the real screen rather than generated as a fresh page of invented parts. That’s the difference between reviewing a change to the product and reviewing something that merely resembles it.

⚠️ States we’d have got to last

Building this way surfaces the states nobody has got to yet, the ones that are neither an error nor progress. More than once they’ve changed the direction we took rather than just how quickly we got there.

The anatomy of one exploration

Problem

Three things were true at once, and each made the others worse.

Exploration was expensive, so it stayed narrow. Drawing screens in Figma was never the problem; prototyping them is. Every state is another screen - loading, empty, error, offline, locked - drawn, linked and kept in step by hand. Three directions across five states is fifteen screens, and a change to one is a change to all. So a feature got one direction, and the argument happened over a frame that couldn’t answer the question.

AI, on its own, made it worse. Write a prompt, spin, mostly miss. The output was always nearly right - a colour close to ours, a component we don’t have, a locked state invented from scratch - and you can’t decide anything from a screen that is approximately the product. It wasn’t the tool. Give a capable model nothing of ours to work from and you get something plausible and generic.

And when I did get it working, it worked for one person. It took a repo, a terminal, git and a feel for how an agent reads a codebase, and none of that is a designer’s job. AI that makes one designer faster while the other six wait for them isn’t a gain for a team; it’s a bottleneck with a demo. The first two problems are about what the AI has to work with. The third is about who gets to use it, and it turned out to be most of the work.

The foundation: something true for the AI to build with

The first job was giving the agent our system instead of its guesses. iOS has no browsable component gallery - SwiftUI previews live in Xcode, which is not a place a designer goes - so I mirrored the iOS design system in React, extracted from the Swift source with Claude Code: colours, spacing, type, and each component’s variants, states and animation timings.

A library tells an agent which components exist, not which one is the wrong choice for the screen in front of it. So each component ships a contract: a Markdown file beside the code - use when, don’t use when, content rules, anti-patterns - and where the two disagree, the Markdown wins. None of that was written down before. It lived in designers’ heads, and it’s exactly what an agent needs, because an agent doesn’t hedge and doesn’t ask.

MD Button.md ## Use when / Don’t use when - **Don’t use** for navigation between tabs or list rows. - **Don’t use** for playback controls — playback has its own components, with different sizing and hit areas. ## Anti-patterns - **Don’t add a `disabled` boolean.** The iOS API models disabled as a *style* ; duplicating it as a prop lets the two disagree. - Pressed state drops to `0.1` opacity over `200ms` . This is unusually aggressive — it is the intended feel, so don’t “fix” it.

One component’s rules, in a file an agent can read

The system has a test. Open a fresh agent session with no history, give it one prompt - a list screen for locked content - and audit what comes back. Right components? Tokens throughout? Both themes? Every failure is a documentation bug, not a model failure: find the missing rule, write it into the contract, run it again. The same rule turned out to apply to people, and it comes back later.

The last piece was Pages. Once the team was exploring, the screens their ideas sat on drifted: every prototype rebuilt the existing screen first, and every rebuild lost something. Five ended up with a nav bar 20 points taller than the app’s, because there was no correct version to copy. So the lab grew Production: every screen in the app rebuilt as it is, on iPhone, iPad and Android, its source named, and not allowed to improve the screen even where the screen is wrong. A prototype now starts from a Page, so what differs between directions is the idea, not the screen underneath.

The For you tab as a Page: one truth, every face - iOS 26, iOS 18, Android, light and dark, and the Danish market - and the rest of the app in the sidebar

Then it became a place to explore

In code a state is a prop, not another screen, and that single difference is what makes exploring several directions in all their states affordable. So the lab grew a harness, built around the argument rather than the screen. Each exploration has two sets of controls: one switches the design option, the other the state - offline, error, loading, locked. Any option can be seen in any state, which a static mockup can’t do, and Compare puts them all on screen at once.

An exploration in the harness: every option in one menu, with its bet beside it

Each option carries a bet: what it’s good for and what it costs, written next to it. Not a description of the layout - an argument, with the trade-off named. Options that lost keep their place with the reason beside them, because it’s easier to agree something is too loud once you can see it, and easier still not to have the argument again in three months.

Downloading in the player Treatment The bet

What the screen draws today, finished: the icon’s own circle becomes a progress ring, and nothing else moves. No layout shift, no reserved space. Its cost is legibility - at that size a ring reads as “something is happening”, not as “42%, about a minute left”. If downloading is meant to sit in the background, this is right and the rest are over-design.

Every prototype also carries two lists. Needs a system decision collects the gaps the exploration ran into; Still unverified lists everything in the screen we made up. Both are part of the work rather than an appendix to it. A prototype that hides what it guessed is worse than one that stops and asks.

The states we’d have got to last

This is where it stopped being only a faster way to do the same work. Downloading a podcast has a state most download UIs skip: the listener taps download on mobile data with mobile-data downloads off, and nothing happens. The control ended up with six states rather than four, and the waiting state is the one that generates the support ticket.

Waiting

Tapped on mobile data with mobile-data downloads switched off. Not an error and not progress - and the one that generates the support ticket, because the listener tapped download, nothing happened, and no screen says why.

The other was a toggle on the show page: keep the newest episodes on this phone, on Wi-Fi, and they play without a signal. The download button on each episode stays; this sits above it, for the shows you never want to be without. One decision instead of a chore every week, and the kind of thing that only shows up when a direction is built out properly rather than drawn.

Example of a great suggestion that we kept

I don’t read that as the model being clever. The house rules say build the unhappy states; the design doc says a screen isn’t finished until loading, empty, error and locked exist. A rule I’d written got applied further than I’d applied it myself, because state exploration is tedious and humans do it last. Write the intent down properly and it holds you to it too.

Making it usable by someone who has never written code

Everything above worked, for me. The people it was for are designers who have never opened Terminal, don’t know what a branch is, and shouldn’t have to. Most of the work since has been re-designing the process for them, and the test was simple: could someone set up and build their first prototype without me in the room?

The whole workflow is four commands

I cut the process to what a designer actually does and made each step one command: /start opens the lab, gets everyone’s latest work and puts you on your own branch; /new-prototype takes the brief; /save commits and pushes; /submit checks the build and opens the pull request, written from the prototype itself. Git still happens. The designer never types it. Each command is a playbook Claude follows, so when a step trips someone, the fix goes in the playbook and nobody hits it again.

The skills, as the Guide lists them: what each one does, how it starts, and what to say

Briefing it like a designer, not prompting it like an engineer

The command that matters most is the one that builds. Getting a good result out of an agent used to mean knowing what to ask for, which is the expertise I least wanted the team to need. So /new-prototype asks instead. Type an idea in a sentence, or paste the whole brief, and it comes back with at most two rounds of multiple-choice questions: three readings of the idea, from one close to your words to two you didn’t say, with “suggest bolder ones” as a fourth; where in the app someone meets it; which screen it starts from. Then it sketches each direction as one screen, side by side in Compare, and stops. Only the direction you pick gets built out, with what was dropped written into the notes.

That order is the point. Building three directions across five states when two get dropped on first sight is where the time and the tokens went. Sketch first, react, then build.

Compare: three answers to the same question, side by side, with the connection switched off for all of them

A Guide that does the typing

The lab carries its own Guide, written for someone who has never opened Terminal. It started as a manual: eleven chapters, read top to bottom, that told you what to type. It’s now a workflow: a one-time setup, then the four steps drawn as a stepper across the top of every page, so you always know which one you’re on.

What changed most is that it stopped asking people to type. Every step has a Copy button for the exact command, because retyping is where a branch name gets mistyped, and an Open in Claude button that opens the app with the prompt already in the box and the lab folder chosen. Setup is two downloads and one button. When something goes wrong, Message Alex on Teams opens a chat with the message already drafted - which step, and a space for what Claude said - so asking for help costs one click.

The Guide’s Build step, old and new: a chapter to read became a step to click

The details are where people get stuck, so that’s where the writing went: it works on Windows as well as a Mac, needs no administrator password, and has chapters for the words you’ll hear, for looking at someone else’s work, and for when something goes wrong. The Guide ends with a rule for itself: if a step tripped you, it will trip the next person, and the fix belongs on the page. The cold-start test, pointed at people.

You can’t break it, and that’s enforced

The line in the Guide that does the most work is near the top: you can’t break it. For someone who has never used git, that matters more than any instruction, so it isn’t a promise, it’s built in. Everyone works on their own branch; nothing reaches the published lab until it’s reviewed. Claude itself is fenced: a hook stops it touching anything platform-owned without asking, and refuses to hand-edit generated files at all. The build fails on a literal colour or font, so a prototype can’t quietly drift off-system.

Approval was the other bottleneck. Every pull request needs one approval and a passing build, and any designer’s approval merges an exploration; only the foundations every prototype sits on need mine, because a change there moves everyone else’s screens. In practice the reviews have all been mine so far - reviewing each other is the next habit to form. Merging publishes the lab, so an exploration is a link, never an export, and never out of date because it is the repo.

From a blank Mac to the whole team, with no step that waits for me

Designing the lab itself

The look is the Bauer AI team’s design system - their canvas, glass, type and components, used as they ship - so the lab sits with the rest of Bauer’s AI tools and I don’t maintain a visual language of my own. Everything else is mine: what the lab does, how it is laid out, how it moves.

The options used to sit in a column beside the frame and took a third of a laptop window. They are now one bar over it, so the screen under review is as big as the window allows and the option and state chips are always in reach. The bar glides between screens, the view controls merge and split with the window, and the theme switch previews before it spreads. One rule holds all of it: the chrome is Bauer’s, the screen under review is Rayo’s, and nothing of theirs reaches inside the frame.

Where the team is with it

Everyone on the team has set up from the Guide, without a session with me, and the buttons get used - the one that opens Claude with the message typed, and the Teams one when something stops. The first prototype through the sketch-first flow is an offline-listening exploration: four directions sketched, three built out across the whole journey, the fourth and the reason it was dropped written into the notes.

It’s also finding what the design system is missing faster than one person reading it could. The gap lists became a channel: an exploration hits a gap, names it in the frame, and the system grows. Gradient tokens exist because a paywall prototype asked for them.

01 An exploration hits a gap “No gradient tokens exist” - on a product whose signature device is a full-bleed gradient hero. → 02 It goes on the notes, not in a backlog Named under “Needs a system decision”, in the frame, next to the thing that needed it. → 03 The system grows Gradient tokens now exist - sampled from the Figma exports, with their uncertainty written down.

A gap list is a feedback channel

The audit file now runs to twelve sections, and the later ones are all things found by using the system - no motion, elevation or blur primitives; a secondary text colour that doesn’t survive a tint; a button with no nav-bar size. Each one is a question the design system now has to answer, raised by something real rather than in the abstract.

What’s next

Reviewing each other. The rule is live; the habit isn’t yet. The next thing I’m watching for is an exploration merged on another designer’s approval, not mine.

Designing together. Designing with AI is still a solo activity. Sharing the result is solved - a prototype is a link - but the session where the options get generated happens between one designer and one agent, and the feedback happens somewhere else, detached from the thing it’s about. There’s nowhere to pin a comment to the exact state you disagree with, and no way to pick up a colleague’s direction and take it somewhere new. The bets and gap lists were a first step towards the argument living inside the work. The next is making room for other people’s arguments there too.

Beyond the design team. Anyone who can describe a screen can now get a real one built from the real system: an engineer sketching a feature, a product manager putting an idea in front of people instead of describing it. The platform is open to them; whether they take it up is the interesting question.

Real listeners. Everything so far has been for internal decisions. A prototype made of the real system with its states already built is most of the way to being testable; the form that takes isn’t decided yet.

Outcome

The team works in it. Making a prototype is faster - the days spent drawing and linking Figma screens are gone - and that’s the speed that matters, because it’s the cost that kept exploration narrow. We can now see many ways of doing the same thing side by side, each with its argument attached, before anyone commits. The downloads work ran through three destinations for downloaded episodes, and the one we took forward won on grounds the exploration made visible.

What I’d take from it is more specific than “AI makes design faster”. The models were never the constraint. The constraint was that they had nothing of ours to build with, and then that only one of us could use them. Give an agent the real system and its rules, and the work is something you can decide from. Take yourself out of the loop - the Guide, the commands, the guardrails, the baseline - and it’s something the whole team can decide from.

Faster to prototype, so more of us explore more of the options before we choose.
